# Creatine supplementation and cognitive performance: evidence appraisal

**Verdict: suggests_with_uncertainty** for the claim that creatine supplementation improves cognitive performance (most specifically memory) relative to placebo in adults. The suggestion is confined to memory in older or sleep-deprived adults; for general cognition in healthy young adults the pooled evidence is null.

## What was and was not searched

Four searches were run: PubMed reviews (60 hits, 25 taken), PubMed trials (179 hits, only 25 taken), PubMed papers since 2023 (1,929 hits, 15 taken) and an OpenAlex citation-sorted search that returned mostly off-topic cardiology papers. The planned observational, Europe PMC and Semantic Scholar searches were not run. No full text was available, so everything here is abstract-only. A 2024 meta-analysis [R5, Xu 2024] appears only as its erratum, with no results, and published comments on the two main syntheses [R7, R1] are missing, so criticisms of them could not be weighed. After screening, 19 of 76 records were relevant.

## What the evidence shows

The best synthesis for memory is a 2023 meta-analysis of 8 randomised trials [R7, Prokopidis 2023]. It reports a small pooled improvement versus placebo (SMD 0.29, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.53) with substantial heterogeneity (I2 66%). The benefit was concentrated in adults aged 66-76 (SMD 0.88, 95% CI 0.22 to 1.55, I2 83%); in 11-31 year olds it was essentially zero (SMD 0.03, 95% CI -0.14 to 0.20). This supersedes a 2018 review of the same trials [R11, Avgerinos 2018] and overlaps heavily with a 2024 review that read the same literature more sceptically, calling the cognitive results equivocal even though supplementation does raise brain creatine [R6, McMorris 2024]. These three count as one body of evidence.

Later trials do not strengthen it much. The largest and best-designed, a pre-registered crossover study of 123 adults taking 5 g/day for six weeks [R20, Sandkuhler 2023], found only a borderline working-memory signal (p = 0.064, partial eta-squared 0.029), nothing on reasoning or eight exploratory tasks, and more side effects on creatine (RR 4.25). It did not replicate the reasoning gains reported in a 45-person vegetarian trial two decades earlier [R25, Rae 2003], and vegetarians did not benefit more than omnivores, unlike in an earlier 128-woman trial [R22, Benton 2011]. In older adults, a one-week trial of 32 people reported gains on nearly every task [R23, McMorris 2007], but a 24-week four-arm trial of 56 older women found no cognitive change at all [R21, Alves 2013]. A 2026 review of six older-adult studies counted five as positive, but four were cross-sectional dietary surveys and three were rated "poor" [R1, Marshall 2026]. A 12-week trial in 64 middle-aged adults reported improvements in "selected markers" of memory while labelling them exploratory [R14, Chun 2026]. No trials exist in people with dementia or mild cognitive impairment [R28, Whitford 2026].

The most consistent signal is under acute sleep deprivation: a single 0.2 g/kg dose reduced deterioration on logical, numerical and vigilance tasks by "up to 12%" in 29 volunteers [R13, Gordji-Nejad 2026], and a week of 20 g/day improved a central executive task at 36 hours awake [R24, McMorris 2007]. A 2026 review found only five such studies [R2, Williams 2026].

Tiny trials in athletes or with other creatine forms [R16, Ben Maaoui 2025; R19, Mabrey 2024; R12, Korovljev 2026; R18, Wu 2025] report scattered positive tests among many outcomes and add little. In a survey of 2,178 users, 64.4% of those taking creatine for cognition perceived no cognitive benefit [R26, Burridge 2026].

## Certainty

Memory: **low** (small trials, heterogeneity, CI near zero). Reasoning and reaction time: **very low** (non-replication, tiny samples, mixed formulations). Sleep deprivation: **low** (consistent direction, few small studies). Older adults: **low** (mostly fair/poor studies, longest trial null). Real-world effectiveness: not demonstrated.

## What would settle it

A pre-registered, multi-site trial of creatine monohydrate versus placebo for 12 or more weeks in several hundred adults, stratified by age, with brain creatine measured and one pre-specified memory outcome. A null result with the confidence interval excluding SMD 0.2 in older adults would overturn the suggestion; a replicated SMD of about 0.3 or more would firm it up. The full 2024 meta-analysis [R5] could also move the estimate.
